# Academic

## 1. Purpose and Scope

Prose applies this transform within the `academic` override scope assigned by the Writing Style Map. It expresses established research content so readers can understand the methods, interpret the findings, and follow scholarly attribution. The Prose Rewrite Constraints and the Map's directives continue to apply.

Use the Map's Language Profile to determine disciplinary conventions and assumed reader knowledge. Make unfamiliar research terminology intelligible using the explanatory context already available in the Draft.

## 2. Writing Rules

Apply these rules to the research functions already present in the Draft.

**Claim expression.** Choose research verbs such as *assume*, *estimate*, *observe*, *derive*, *suggest*, and *demonstrate* to express the established nature of each claim. Match qualifications to its actual uncertainty; academic tone does not require making every statement tentative.

### 2.1 Methodology and Methods

**Research description.** Describe what was done, to which material, and by which method. Replace a narration of the writer's activity with a direct account of the research operation. Where the Draft supplies a methodological rationale, express why the method serves its purpose. Present implementation details as research descriptions rather than commands to the reader.

**Subject and tense.** Let the sentence's focus determine whether the researcher, sample, model, or process is its subject. Usually use past tense for completed procedures and present tense for general methodological properties; keep proposed work distinguishable from completed work. Follow the assigned disciplinary conventions without imposing a uniform voice or tense.

**Before:**

> We first divided the participants into two groups based on whether they had prior programming experience. We then gave both groups the same debugging task and recorded how long each participant took to complete it. We decided to use this task because it required participants to identify both syntactic and logical errors. After collecting the data, we used a mixed-effects model to examine the relationship between prior experience and completion time.

**After:**

> Participants were divided into groups with and without prior programming experience and completed the same debugging task. The task required identification of both syntactic and logical errors. Completion time was recorded for each participant, and its association with prior experience was estimated using a mixed-effects model.

### 2.2 Findings and Evidence

**Finding-led expression.** In Results, state the finding before or with the figure or table callout. Realize this emphasis within the established Semantic Units and Presentation Order. Give the reader the substantive observation rather than an announcement that results are available.

**Evidence in the sentence.** Attach the relevant values, comparison, and uncertainty to the finding they describe. For qualitative findings, make clear which theme or observation an existing excerpt illustrates. Describe the established pattern rather than narrating every table entry. Use captions to identify what is displayed and explain the supplied labels, quantities, and conditions needed to read it.

**Before:**

> Figure 2 shows the error rates of models A and B under three signal-to-noise conditions. As can be seen from the figure, model A performed better when the signal-to-noise ratio was low. Its mean error was 0.22, whereas the mean error of model B was 0.25, which represents a reduction of 12%. No clear difference was observed under the medium- or high-signal conditions.

**After:**

> Under the low-signal condition, model A reduced mean error by 12% relative to model B (0.22 versus 0.25; Figure 2). The models showed no clear difference under the medium- or high-signal conditions.

### 2.3 Analysis and Interpretation

**Interpretive language.** Express an established explanation, comparison with prior work, or implication directly. Use wording that distinguishes an observed result from an interpretation of it: *is consistent with*, *may reflect*, and *suggests* express different relationships and are not interchangeable academic decorations. Name the specific explanation already given instead of announcing that several explanations are possible.

**Contribution and limitations.** State what the research contributes and what an identified limitation restricts. Replace generic evaluations such as *important* or *promising* with the supplied substantive contribution or implication when the adjective adds nothing. Likewise, express the stated effect of a limitation instead of a detached instruction to interpret everything cautiously.

### 2.4 Attribution and Citation

**Source focus.** Make the author or study prominent when its position or contribution is the focus; foreground the proposition when the finding itself is the focus. Choose reporting verbs that identify the source's actual contribution, such as proposing an account, reporting an observation, or questioning an interpretation.

**Citation placement.** Integrate citations with the clauses they support, using the assigned citation convention. Keep the source's position distinguishable from the current writer's interpretation. When discussing several studies, express their established agreement, disagreement, or contribution to the question rather than substituting a list of names for that discussion.

### 2.5 Summaries

**Abstracts and standalone summaries.** Express the established research question, approach, and answer as compact research statements. Preserve enough named relations, group contrasts, directions, and qualifications to make the answer intelligible without internal codes or figures. Treat the word limit as a ceiling, not a reason to erase essential meaning.

Use descriptive names or supplied expansions where project-internal shorthand would be opaque to a reader encountering the summary alone. Remove process preambles and announcements of what the summary will discuss.

**Conclusions.** Express the established synthesis, contribution, and limits of application rather than repeating the abstract's account of the study. Preserve the study's actual task verb and inference level. Let the substantive conclusion carry the ending instead of adding a generic statement about importance or future promise.
